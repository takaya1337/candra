# Why my app is free

> "He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me."
>
> Thomas Jefferson, letter to Isaac McPherson, 1813

My app is free, and it will stay free, not as a promotion and not because it is worth little. It is free because of something true about the kind of thing it is, a truth Jefferson named two hundred years ago and one the study of economics has only sharpened since. This is the short version of it.

---

## 1. Non-rival goods

Make a chair, and there is one chair. If you take it I no longer have it, and to earn again I must cut more wood and build another, and that next chair will cost me about what the first one did, so the effort and the earning are bound together, and to be paid twice I must work twice.

A file breaks that binding. If I give you a copy I still keep mine, and we can both hold it at once, and so can a million others, and the next copy costs almost nothing to make. Economists call a thing of this kind non-rival, meaning that your use of it does not use it up. Samuelson gave the idea its modern form in 1954, though Jefferson had already given it his candle nearly a century and a half before, and it is the deepest fact about digital work, the one that most quarrels about price and ownership go wrong by ignoring.

---

## 2. Return asymmetry

The effort to make a thing is finite. There is a first day and a day on which it is ready, and between them a real and countable cost, and that cost deserves to be met, and met well.

But once it has been met, the copying is free and need never stop, so a book can sell a thousand times what it cost to write, and then a thousand times again, with nothing further owed to its making. Earnings that go on after the effort is paid for, that call forth no new work because the work is already done, have a name in economics, and the name is rent, not wages and not the profit on a thing built, but rent, collected on something that exists already and copies itself for nothing. The strangeness you may feel about digital goods is exactly this, that the cost is spent once and the toll can run forever.

---

## 3. What a copyright was meant to be

None of this means that the maker should go unpaid. It means only that we should be honest about the instrument.

A copyright is not an old thing, nor a natural kind of property. It is a monopoly, granted on purpose by law, for a single reason, which was to fund the finite effort of making, after which the work was meant to pass into common hands. The people who built it said as much, plainly: Britain's first copyright law, in 1710, called itself "An Act for the Encouragement of Learning," and the American Constitution grants the right "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts" and only "for limited Times." And the people who later argued over its term said it more plainly still. Thomas Macaulay, speaking in the House of Commons on 5 February 1841 against lengthening it, called it "a tax on readers for the purpose of giving a bounty to writers," and he warned that "monopoly is an evil. For the sake of the good we must submit to the evil; but the evil ought not to last a day longer than is necessary for the purpose of securing the good."

A bargain, in other words, and one with a term, the effort funded first and the knowledge set free after. Read that line of his again, and then look at how long the toll actually runs today. The gap between what the instrument was built for and what it has since become is the whole of the matter.

---

## 4. On my hypocrisy

So this is not the claim that nothing should be paid for, which would be as dishonest as its opposite. Stewart Brand put the real shape of it in 1984, and wrote it down in 1987: that information wants to be free and information also wants to be expensive, both at once, and that "that tension will not go away."

My answer to that tension is a line drawn through it. Where the effort is finished and the copies cost nothing, I do not charge, and this app sits on that side of the line, free, for anyone, for as long as it runs. Where the effort is real and ongoing, in the labor of building a thing to someone's own need and keeping it alive and answering for it, I charge, as Nero Wolfe would put it, an exorbitant fee for my services, since I too would like to live a comfortable life, though I wish to do so without worrying over my probity, which I would certainly damage were I to obtain my wealth without earning it. That is work being done, and not rent being collected. Payment should follow effort that is actually happening, and it should not follow a copy that makes itself.

That is the whole of the principle, and this app is simply the principle kept. You are holding the free side of the bargain, offered the way it was always meant to be offered, and what you do with the idea now is yours, for Jefferson's candle gives you its light without losing any of its own.
